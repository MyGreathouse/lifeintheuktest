The Law and Your Role

This part covers the law, the police, the courts, your rights, tax, driving and your role in the community. Many answers here are short, practical rules, so the notes are written to be learned and checked quickly.

The law

  • Everyone is treated equally under the law. Criminal law: crimes, usually investigated by police, punished by the courts. Civil law: disputes between people or groups (housing, consumer rights, employment, debt).
  • Crimes: carrying a weapon (even for self-defence), buying or selling drugs, racial harassment, selling tobacco or alcohol to under-18s, smoking in enclosed public places, drinking in alcohol-free zones.
  • Ages: 18 to buy alcohol or tobacco, but 16 to drink beer or wine with a meal with an adult.

Police

  • Protect life and property, keep the peace, prevent and detect crime. They are independent of the government, headed by chief constables. Police and crime commissioners in England and Wales were first elected in November 2012. PCSOs support officers on patrol.
  • Police must obey the law too. Complain to the chief constable or an independent body.
  • Terrorism and extremism: report anyone trying to recruit you to the police.

The courts

  • Judges interpret law and the government cannot interfere with them.
  • Minor crime: magistrates' court (England, Wales, NI), justice of the peace court (Scotland). Magistrates are unpaid and unqualified but trained. In Northern Ireland a district judge hears minor cases.
  • Serious crime: crown court with a judge and jury (England, Wales, NI). Scotland uses sheriff courts, and the High Court for the most serious cases like murder.
  • Jury: 12 in England, Wales and NI, 15 in Scotland, picked from the electoral register. Scotland also has not proven. Jury service is compulsory if you are summoned and eligible. Ages are 18 to 70, or 18 to 75 in England and Wales.
  • Youth courts: ages 10 to 17, with up to three trained magistrates, no public, and no names published. Scotland uses the children's hearing system, and Northern Ireland youth conferencing.
  • Civil courts: county courts (sheriff court in Scotland), the High Court, and the Court of Session in Scotland for larger claims.
  • Small claims: less than £10,000 in England and Wales, £3,000 in Scotland and Northern Ireland. Informal, no lawyer needed.
  • Solicitors give legal advice. Citizens Advice can point you to one.

Rights

  • Roots: Magna Carta, Habeas Corpus Act, Bill of Rights 1689.
  • The UK signed the European Convention on Human Rights in 1950. The Human Rights Act 1998 made it part of UK law. Rights include life, no torture, no slavery, liberty, fair trial, thought, conscience, religion and expression.
  • Equality: protection from unfair treatment based on age, disability, sex, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sexuality or marital status.
  • Illegal: domestic violence, FGM, forced marriage. Arranged marriages are fine if both people consent.

Tax and insurance

  • Income tax: collected by HMRC. Employees pay through PAYE. Self-employed people file a self assessment return.
  • National Insurance: pays for the state pension, NHS and benefits. You get a National Insurance number around your 16th birthday, or you apply as a newcomer, and you can start work without one.

Driving

  • You must be 17 to drive a car and 16 to ride a moped. You need a licence, which is valid until 70 and then renewed every 3 years.
  • A foreign licence is good for 12 months.
  • You need motor insurance, vehicle tax (registered with the DVLA), and an MOT every year once the vehicle is over 3 years old.

Your role in the community

  • Responsibilities: obey the law, respect others' rights, treat people fairly, behave responsibly, protect your family, protect the environment, work and provide for your family, help others, vote.
  • Neighbours: introduce yourself, respect privacy, keep noise down, put bins out only on collection day.
  • Ways to help: jury service, helping in schools (parent teacher associations), school governors (18+, no upper age limit), political parties and canvassing, volunteering (hospitals, police special constable, magistrate), blood and organ donation, charities (Red Cross, NSPCC, Crisis, Shelter), the National Citizen Service for 16 and 17 year olds, recycling and shopping locally.

Memory anchors

  • 12 and 15: "12 for England, Wales and Ireland's north, 15 for the Scots." Scotland adds "not proven".
  • 10 to 17 is youth court. Magistrates handle minor cases, crown courts serious ones.
  • Money limits: "10 for England and Wales, 3 for the rest."
  • PAYE for employees, self assessment for the self-employed.
  • 17 car, 16 moped, 70 renew, 3 years MOT.
  • 1950 signed, 1998 made law.
  • Illegal three: domestic violence, FGM, forced marriage.

Practice questions with answers

Ten questions from this chapter, with the correct answer and explanation shown. The full bank has 90 questions on this topic.

1. Which court in England and Wales deals with the most serious criminal cases?

  • A. The Crown Court
  • B. The Small Claims Court
  • C. The County Court
  • D. The Magistrates' Court

Answer: A. The Crown Court

Serious criminal cases in England and Wales are tried in the Crown Court before a judge and jury.

2. In Scotland, minor criminal cases are heard in:

  • A. The County Court
  • B. The Magistrates' Court
  • C. The Justice of the Peace Court
  • D. The Crown Court

Answer: C. The Justice of the Peace Court

Scotland has a separate legal system; minor cases are heard in Justice of the Peace Courts, with more serious cases in the Sheriff Court or High Court.

3. The police in the UK are expected to be:

  • A. Above the law
  • B. Accountable and subject to the same laws as everyone else
  • C. Controlled by the governing party
  • D. Exempt from complaints

Answer: B. Accountable and subject to the same laws as everyone else

Police officers must obey the law themselves and are accountable for their conduct.

4. National Insurance contributions help pay for:

  • A. Company profits
  • B. Private school fees
  • C. Foreign aid only
  • D. State benefits and the state pension

Answer: D. State benefits and the state pension

National Insurance contributions fund state benefits, the NHS and the state pension.

5. Which of these is an example of a criminal offence in the UK?

  • A. Disagreeing with a government policy
  • B. Attending a peaceful protest
  • C. Joining a trade union
  • D. Selling alcohol to someone under 18

Answer: D. Selling alcohol to someone under 18

Selling alcohol to a person under 18 is a criminal offence. Peaceful protest and union membership are lawful.

6. Which independent body deals with serious complaints about the police in England and Wales?

  • A. Ofcom
  • B. The Crown Prosecution Service
  • C. The Independent Office for Police Conduct
  • D. The Police Federation

Answer: C. The Independent Office for Police Conduct

The Independent Office for Police Conduct oversees the complaints system and investigates the most serious complaints and incidents involving the police.

7. How is most NHS care in the UK paid for?

  • A. By the local council only
  • B. By monthly insurance payments
  • C. By fees paid at every appointment
  • D. It is paid for through taxes and is free at the point of use

Answer: D. It is paid for through taxes and is free at the point of use

The National Health Service is funded mainly through taxes and National Insurance. Most care, including seeing a GP, is free at the point of use.

8. What is the difference between criminal and civil law?

  • A. Criminal law applies only to the rich
  • B. Civil law applies only to the police
  • C. There is no difference
  • D. Criminal law deals with crimes punished by the courts; civil law settles disputes between individuals or groups

Answer: D. Criminal law deals with crimes punished by the courts; civil law settles disputes between individuals or groups

Examples of civil law include housing, consumer rights, employment and debt.

9. Who sets local police priorities and the local policing budget in England and Wales?

  • A. Police and crime commissioners
  • B. Chief constables
  • C. Local magistrates
  • D. The prime minister

Answer: A. Police and crime commissioners

They were first publicly elected in November 2012 and appoint the local chief constable.

10. What age range of people are normally tried in a youth court?

  • A. 14 to 21
  • B. 16 to 25
  • C. 5 to 12
  • D. 10 to 17

Answer: D. 10 to 17

Members of the public are not allowed in, and the young person's name cannot be published.
