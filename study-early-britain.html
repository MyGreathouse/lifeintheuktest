Early Britain to the Stuarts

Chapter 3 of 7. Free study notes for the Life in the UK Test, written in our own words from the official handbook.

Practise this chapter

Home › Study guide › Early Britain to the Stuarts

This is the longest stretch of the history chapter, and the test returns to it often. Read it as a story of invaders and rulers, not a list of dates: Romans, Anglo-Saxons and Vikings, Normans, the long struggle between kings and Parliament, then the Tudors and Stuarts. Every group below is a set of facts the handbook states and the test can ask about.

Before the Romans

  • Stone Age: hunter-gatherers; Britain was joined to Europe by land until about 10,000 years ago. The first farmers arrived about 6,000 years ago and built Stonehenge (Wiltshire). Skara Brae (Orkney) is the best-preserved prehistoric village.
  • Bronze Age: about 4,000 years ago, with skilled metalworkers and round barrows.
  • Iron Age: round houses and hill forts (Maiden Castle, Dorset), Celtic languages, and the first British coins.

Romans (AD 43 to 410)

  • Julius Caesar's invasion in 55 BC failed. Claudius invaded in AD 43 and Rome held most of Britain for about 400 years.
  • Boudicca, queen of the Iceni, resisted. Hadrian's Wall marked the northern edge, and Scotland was never conquered.
  • Rome brought roads, public buildings and law. The first Christian communities appeared in the 3rd and 4th centuries.

Anglo-Saxons and Vikings

  • After the Romans left in AD 410, Jutes, Angles and Saxons settled, and their languages are the basis of English. The Sutton Hoo ship burial is the famous find.
  • Missionaries brought Christianity: Patrick and Columba from Ireland, and Augustine from Rome (first Archbishop of Canterbury).
  • Vikings raided from AD 789 and settled in the Danelaw. Alfred the Great defeated them. Canute was a Danish king of England. Kenneth MacAlpin united the Scots.

Normans, 1066

  • William of Normandy beat Harold at Hastings. The Bayeux Tapestry records it. The Domesday Book listed land and people. It was the last successful invasion of England.

Middle Ages

  • Wales: Edward I's Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) took Wales into the English crown, and castles were built to hold it.
  • Scotland: Robert the Bruce won at Bannockburn (1314).
  • Wars abroad: the Crusades, and the Hundred Years War with France, including Agincourt (1415).
  • Feudalism: the king gave land to lords in exchange for military service, and peasants (serfs) worked the land and could not leave.
  • Black Death (1348): about a third of the population died. Wages rose and new social classes appeared.
  • Magna Carta (1215): King John accepted that the king is subject to the law. Parliament grew, with Lords and elected Commons. Scotland's parliament had three estates.
  • Law: English judges built common law from precedent. Scotland's law was written down.
  • Language and culture: Norman French and Anglo-Saxon merged into English. Chaucer wrote the Canterbury Tales, and Caxton printed books.
  • Wars of the Roses (1455 to 1485): Lancaster (red) against York (white), ended at Bosworth, when Henry Tudor became Henry VII.

Tudors and Stuarts

  • Henry VIII broke with Rome and founded the Church of England. His six wives are listed in the anchors below. Wales was united with England in his reign.
  • Edward VI was a strong Protestant, and the Book of Common Prayer dates from his reign. Mary I was Catholic and persecuted Protestants (Bloody Mary).
  • Elizabeth I found a middle way on religion and beat the Spanish Armada (1588). Drake sailed round the world in the Golden Hind. Shakespeare wrote for the Globe.
  • Scotland: a Protestant Church of Scotland in 1560. Mary, Queen of Scots was held prisoner by Elizabeth for 20 years, then executed.
  • James VI of Scotland became James I of England. The King James Bible came from his reign. Protestants were settled in Ulster (the plantations).
  • Charles I believed in the divine right of kings. He ruled without Parliament for 11 years. Civil war began in 1642 (Cavaliers for the king, Roundheads for Parliament). He was executed in 1649.
  • Cromwell ruled as Lord Protector until 1658, then the monarchy returned in 1660 with Charles II.
  • Charles II's reign: plague in 1665 and the Great Fire in 1666. Wren rebuilt St Paul's, and Pepys kept a diary. Habeas corpus was passed in 1679. The Royal Society was founded and Newton worked on gravity.
  • Glorious Revolution (1688): the Catholic James II was replaced by William of Orange and Mary. William beat James at the Boyne (1690). Jacobites supported James.
  • Bill of Rights (1689): Parliament controls taxes and the monarch must be Protestant. Elections at least every 3 years (now 5). Whigs and Tories began, and the press was freed in 1695.
  • Act of Union (1707): England and Scotland form Great Britain. Scotland keeps its own legal and education systems and Church.

Exam traps: false or true?

Tempting answerThe truth
Julius Caesar conquered BritainHis 55 BC invasion failed. Claudius succeeded in AD 43.
The Romans conquered all of BritainScotland was never conquered, hence Hadrian's Wall.
The Normans took Scotland tooThey conquered England and parts of Wales, but not Scotland.
Bannockburn was an English victoryIt was a Scottish victory under Robert the Bruce.
The Magna Carta made England a democracyIt said even the king is under the law. Democracy came much later.
The Wars of the Roses ended with a Stuart kingThey ended at Bosworth (1485) and began the Tudor dynasty.
Elizabeth I's religious policy was fierce persecutionShe sought a balance and did not probe people's private beliefs.
England became a republic under Charles IIThe republic (the Commonwealth) came after Charles I was executed, and Charles II restored the monarchy in 1660.
The Glorious Revolution involved a big war in EnglandIt was almost bloodless in England. The Boyne was fought in Ireland.
The Act of Union ended Scotland's legal systemScotland kept its own law, education and Church.

Memory anchors

  • Henry VIII's wives: "Divorced, beheaded, died; divorced, beheaded, survived." (Catherine of Aragon, Anne Boleyn, Jane Seymour, Anne of Cleves, Catherine Howard, Catherine Parr.)
  • Romans: "Caesar fails, Claudius stays" (55 BC then AD 43).
  • 1066, 1215, 1314, 1348, 1415: read them as a story. Conquest, charter, Scottish win, plague, Agincourt.
  • Red and white roses: Lancaster red, York white, and the Tudor rose joins both.
  • "1642 war, 1649 axe, 1660 back": Civil war starts, the king is executed, the monarchy returns.
  • "88 then 89": Glorious Revolution in 1688, Bill of Rights in 1689.

Remember it like this. Take it in six steps: Romans, then Anglo-Saxons and Vikings, then Normans (1066), then the medieval power struggles (Magna Carta, Wales, Scotland), then the Tudors, then the Stuarts. Learn the traps table last, because each wrong answer there is a real mix-up candidates make.

Practice questions with answers

Ten questions from this chapter, with the correct answer and explanation shown. The full bank has 83 questions on this topic.

1. Skara Brae, the best-preserved prehistoric village in northern Europe, is located on:

  • A. Orkney
  • B. Jersey
  • C. Anglesey
  • D. The Isle of Wight

Answer: A. Orkney

Skara Brae is on Orkney, off the north coast of Scotland, and dates from the Stone Age.

2. In which year did the Battle of Hastings take place?

  • A. 1605
  • B. 1415
  • C. 1215
  • D. 1066

Answer: D. 1066

The Battle of Hastings took place in 1066, when William of Normandy defeated King Harold II.

3. Which document, sealed in 1215, established that the king was subject to the law?

  • A. The Act of Union
  • B. The Bill of Rights
  • C. The Petition of Right
  • D. Magna Carta

Answer: D. Magna Carta

Magna Carta, agreed by King John in 1215, established the principle that the monarch was not above the law.

4. Who became the first Tudor king after victory at the Battle of Bosworth Field in 1485?

  • A. Edward IV
  • B. Richard III
  • C. Henry VII
  • D. Henry VIII

Answer: C. Henry VII

Henry Tudor defeated Richard III at Bosworth Field in 1485 and became Henry VII, founding the Tudor dynasty.

5. The Gunpowder Plot of 1605 was an attempt to:

  • A. Burn down the Tower of London
  • B. Blow up the Houses of Parliament and kill King James I
  • C. Assassinate Elizabeth I
  • D. Sink the Spanish Armada

Answer: B. Blow up the Houses of Parliament and kill King James I

Guy Fawkes and his fellow conspirators plotted to blow up Parliament and kill James I. The failure is marked each 5 November.

6. When did the first Christian communities begin to appear in Britain?

  • A. Before the Romans arrived
  • B. In the 3rd and 4th centuries AD
  • C. After the Norman conquest
  • D. In the 9th century, with the Vikings

Answer: B. In the 3rd and 4th centuries AD

Christianity first appeared in Roman Britain in the third and fourth centuries AD.

7. In which year did the Vikings first raid Britain?

  • A. AD 43
  • B. AD 789
  • C. AD 410
  • D. AD 1066

Answer: B. AD 789

The Vikings, from Denmark and Norway, first raided coastal towns in AD 789 before settling.

8. Who wrote the Canterbury Tales?

  • A. Samuel Pepys
  • B. William Shakespeare
  • C. Geoffrey Chaucer
  • D. John Milton

Answer: C. Geoffrey Chaucer

Chaucer wrote poems in English about pilgrims travelling to Canterbury and the stories they told.

9. Which monarch's reign saw the Book of Common Prayer written for the Church of England?

  • A. Mary I
  • B. Edward VI
  • C. James I
  • D. Elizabeth I

Answer: B. Edward VI

Edward VI was strongly Protestant. A version of the prayer book is still used in some churches today.

10. In the English Civil War, who were the 'Cavaliers'?

  • A. Supporters of Parliament
  • B. The king's judges
  • C. Scottish soldiers
  • D. Supporters of the king

Answer: D. Supporters of the king

The Cavaliers supported Charles I, and the Roundheads supported Parliament.
