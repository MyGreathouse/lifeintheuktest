Government and Democracy

Chapter 6 of 7. Free study notes for the Life in the UK Test, written in our own words from the official handbook.

Home › Study guide › Government and Democracy

This part covers how the UK is governed: how democracy developed, the monarch, Parliament, government, devolution, voting and international bodies. The test likes the roles of each body, so the groups below are written around who does what.

Development of democracy

  • In 1800 only some men over 21 with property could vote.
  • Chartists (1830s to 1840s) asked for six things: votes for all men, annual elections, equal constituencies, secret ballots, no property qualification for MPs, and pay for MPs.
  • Women over 30 got the vote in 1918. Everyone over 21 got it in 1928. The voting age fell to 18 in 1969.

The constitution

  • A constitution is the set of principles by which a country is governed. The UK's is unwritten, because it is not in one document and the UK never had a revolution that replaced its system.
  • Parts of government: the monarchy, Parliament (Commons and Lords), the prime minister, the Cabinet, the courts, the police, the civil service, and local and devolved government.

The monarch

  • King Charles III has been head of state since September 2022. Prince William is the heir.
  • It is a constitutional monarchy: the monarch does not rule but appoints the government people have chosen, inviting the leader of the largest party (or a coalition) to be prime minister.
  • Ceremonial roles: opening each new parliamentary session with a speech, all Acts in his name, welcoming ambassadors and heads of state.
  • National anthem: God Save the King.
  • Oath of allegiance (or affirmation): faithful and true allegiance to the King, his heirs and successors, according to law.

Parliament

  • The UK is a parliamentary democracy. It is divided into constituencies, and each elects one MP. All MPs form the House of Commons.
  • Commons: the more important chamber because it is elected. The PM and almost all Cabinet ministers sit there.
  • House of Lords: peers are not elected. Before 1958 almost all were hereditary. Since 1958 there have been life peers appointed by the monarch on the PM's advice. Since 1999 hereditary peers lose their automatic seat, and a few are elected by their fellows. The Lords checks and suggests changes to laws, and the Commons can overrule it.
  • The Speaker: chairs Commons debates, is neutral, is chosen by MPs in a secret ballot, and protects the opposition's debating time.
  • Elections: a general election at least every five years. First past the post. When an MP dies or resigns, a by-election follows. If no party has a majority, two parties can form a coalition.
  • You can contact your MP by letter, phone, or at a local surgery.

Government

  • Prime minister: leader of the governing party, lives at 10 Downing Street, with a country house, Chequers. The first PM was Sir Robert Walpole.
  • Cabinet: about 20 senior MPs who meet weekly. Chancellor of the Exchequer (economy), Home Secretary (crime, policing, immigration), Foreign Secretary.
  • Opposition: the second largest party. Its leader runs the shadow cabinet. PM's Questions happen weekly.
  • Parties: Conservative, Labour, Liberal Democrats, and Scottish, Welsh and Northern Irish parties. Independents have no party.
  • Pressure and lobby groups try to influence policy (for example the CBI, Greenpeace, Liberty).
  • Civil service: carries out government policy, is politically neutral and chosen on merit, with values of integrity, honesty, objectivity and impartiality.
  • Local government: elected councils, funded by central government and local taxes. A mayor may be ceremonial or elected. London has 33 local authorities and a mayor. Council elections are in May.

Devolution (since 1997)

  • Scotland: Parliament in Edinburgh (Holyrood), 129 MSPs, set up in 1999, broad powers including law, health and education.
  • Wales: the Senedd in Cardiff Bay, with the handbook figure of 60 members. Welsh Assembly from 1999. Since 2011 it can pass laws on devolved topics without UK approval.
  • Northern Ireland: Assembly at Stormont in Belfast, 90 MLAs, power sharing, set up after the 1998 Good Friday Agreement. It has been suspended several times, and the UK government can suspend all devolved bodies.
  • Reserved to the UK: defence, foreign affairs, social security and most taxation.
  • Hansard records parliamentary debates. The UK has a free press, and broadcasters must be balanced about parties.

Voting

  • Who may vote: adult UK citizens, and Commonwealth and Irish citizens resident in the UK. A fully democratic system since 1928, with the age of 18 from 1969.
  • Be on the electoral register, updated every September or October. Northern Ireland uses individual registration.
  • Polling stations open 7 am to 10 pm. Take photo ID. Fill in the ballot privately. A postal ballot is an option.
  • You may stand for office from 18 if a UK, Irish or Commonwealth citizen. Exceptions include members of the Lords (for the Commons), civil servants, some military and people with certain convictions.

International bodies

  • Commonwealth: 56 members today, voluntary, no power over members, the King is its ceremonial head. Values: democracy, good government, rule of law.
  • Council of Europe: 47 members, human rights, wrote the European Convention on Human Rights.
  • United Nations: more than 190 members, set up after the Second World War. The Security Council has 15 members, and the UK is one of five permanent members.
  • NATO: European and North American countries agreeing to help one another if attacked.

Memory anchors

  • Votes: "30 in 18, 21 in 28, 18 in 69."
  • Members: "129 in Edinburgh, 90 at Stormont, Senedd in Cardiff Bay."
  • Cabinet jobs: Chancellor counts the money, Home Secretary handles homes and crime, Foreign Secretary looks abroad.
  • Lords history: "58 life peers, 99 hereditary gone."
  • Five years, first past the post: most votes wins, and the seat is yours.
  • Commonwealth 56, Council of Europe 47, UN Security Council 5 permanent.
  • Photo ID, 7 till 10.

Practice questions with answers

Ten questions from this chapter, with the correct answer and explanation shown. The full bank has 87 questions on this topic.

1. The UK is best described as a:

  • A. Republic
  • B. Parliamentary democracy with a constitutional monarch
  • C. Military dictatorship
  • D. Absolute monarchy

Answer: B. Parliamentary democracy with a constitutional monarch

The UK is a parliamentary democracy with a constitutional monarch as head of state.

2. What is the maximum period between UK general elections?

  • A. Four years
  • B. Seven years
  • C. Three years
  • D. Five years

Answer: D. Five years

A general election must be held at least every five years.

3. The civil service is expected to be:

  • A. Politically neutral and professional
  • B. Loyal only to one party
  • C. Appointed by the monarch personally
  • D. Elected by the public

Answer: A. Politically neutral and professional

Civil servants are politically neutral professionals who serve the government of the day.

4. You can contact your MP by:

  • A. Writing to them or attending their surgery
  • B. Only through a solicitor
  • C. Only by visiting Buckingham Palace
  • D. You cannot contact MPs

Answer: A. Writing to them or attending their surgery

Constituents can write to their MP, email them, or attend an advice surgery to raise concerns.

5. Who can stand as a candidate in a UK general election?

  • A. Only members of the House of Lords
  • B. Most UK, Irish and qualifying Commonwealth citizens aged 18 or over
  • C. Only people over 40
  • D. Only civil servants

Answer: B. Most UK, Irish and qualifying Commonwealth citizens aged 18 or over

Most people aged 18 or over who are British, Irish or qualifying Commonwealth citizens may stand, subject to some disqualifications.

6. Which government minister is responsible for the UK's finances and the Treasury?

  • A. The Home Secretary
  • B. The Foreign Secretary
  • C. The Chancellor of the Exchequer
  • D. The Speaker

Answer: C. The Chancellor of the Exchequer

The Chancellor of the Exchequer is the senior minister responsible for the economy and public finances, including the annual Budget.

7. How does a person usually become Prime Minister?

  • A. By being appointed by the House of Lords
  • B. By being chosen by the Speaker
  • C. By winning a separate national vote for Prime Minister
  • D. By being the leader of the party that can command a majority in the House of Commons

Answer: D. By being the leader of the party that can command a majority in the House of Commons

After an election, the monarch invites the leader of the party that can command a majority in the Commons to form a government.

8. What does it mean that the UK has a constitutional monarchy?

  • A. The monarch commands the Cabinet
  • B. The monarch chooses MPs
  • C. The monarch does not rule but appoints the government the people have chosen
  • D. The monarch makes all laws

Answer: C. The monarch does not rule but appoints the government the people have chosen

The monarch invites the leader of the party with the most MPs, or of a coalition, to become prime minister.

9. Roughly how many senior MPs does the prime minister appoint to form the Cabinet?

  • A. About 5
  • B. About 20
  • C. About 100
  • D. About 650

Answer: B. About 20

They are ministers in charge of departments and meet usually weekly.

10. What are the core values of the civil service?

  • A. Faith, hope and charity
  • B. Integrity, honesty, objectivity and impartiality
  • C. Wealth, power, loyalty and secrecy
  • D. Profit, speed and obedience

Answer: B. Integrity, honesty, objectivity and impartiality

Civil servants are chosen on merit, are politically neutral and are accountable to ministers.
