What is the Life in the UK Test?

The Life in the UK Test is a computer-based multiple-choice test used as part of the process for settling permanently in the UK (indefinite leave to remain) or becoming a British citizen. It checks whether you have a reasonable working knowledge of British traditions, history, government and everyday life — the kind of knowledge the government expects a settled resident to have.

It is not a language test on its own, though a reasonable standard of English is needed to read and understand the questions in the time given.

Who needs to take it?

Generally, most applicants aged 18 to 65 applying for indefinite leave to remain or British citizenship need to pass it, with some exceptions and exemptions (for example, based on age, certain visa categories, or a recognised long-term physical or mental condition). Exemption rules are specific and change from time to time, so this is one area where checking GOV.UK directly, rather than relying on any third-party summary, genuinely matters.

How is it structured?

The test is 24 multiple-choice questions, and you're given 45 minutes to complete it. Each question has four possible answers, only one of which is correct. Questions are drawn from across the government's official study handbook, covering British values, history, geography, government, and modern life.

What score do you need to pass?

You need to answer at least 18 of the 24 questions correctly — a 75% pass mark. Getting 17 or fewer correct means you don't pass that sitting.

How do you book it?

The test is booked online through the official GOV.UK booking system, at a registered test centre near you, for a fee set by the government (this fee changes from time to time, so check the current amount on GOV.UK rather than trusting a number quoted elsewhere, including here). You'll usually be able to choose from a number of test centres and available time slots.

What do you need to bring?

You'll need valid, acceptable photo identification (commonly a passport, biometric residence permit, or another document from the accepted list) and proof of your current address, exactly as specified in your booking confirmation. Test centres are strict about this — turning up with the wrong documents typically means you can't sit the test and may lose your fee, so read your confirmation email carefully rather than assuming.

What happens at the test centre?

You'll check in, your identity will be verified, and you'll be seated at a computer terminal. The test itself is taken on-screen, is timed automatically, and your result is typically available very shortly after you finish. Staff at the centre will talk you through the process when you arrive if anything is unclear.

What if you don't pass?

You can book and retake the test. There's no limit on the number of attempts, but each attempt is a separate fee and separate booking — so it's worth genuinely preparing before your first sitting rather than treating it as a low-stakes trial run.

What happens after you pass?

You'll receive a pass notification letter, which becomes part of the evidence for your wider application (settlement or citizenship). Keep it safe — you may be asked to provide it later in your application process.

How should you prepare?

The official government handbook is the actual source material the test is drawn from, and reading it properly is the single most important step — no third-party site, including this one, is a substitute for it. Common mistakes candidates make

Confusing "UK" with "Great Britain"

These aren't the same thing — Great Britain doesn't include Northern Ireland. This single mix-up accounts for a surprising number of lost marks. See our What is the UK lesson for the full explanation.

Treating the test as a memory-only exercise

Rote memorisation without understanding tends to fall apart under timed pressure. Understanding why an answer is correct, not just which letter it is, tends to hold up better on the day.

Not practising under time pressure

45 minutes for 24 questions is generous if you know the material, but candidates who've never sat a timed practice test sometimes lose time on early questions and rush the rest. A full 45-minute mock test beforehand fixes this.

Leaving booking to the last minute

Test centre slots can fill up, particularly near your required date. Booking earlier gives you more choice of date, location and preparation time.
