The Empire to Modern Britain

Chapter 4 of 7. Free study notes for the Life in the UK Test, written in our own words from the official handbook.

Home › Study guide › The Empire to Modern Britain

From 1714 to today the chapter moves quickly: the Georgians and the Industrial Revolution, Victorian Britain and the Empire, then two world wars and the changes since 1945. Read the groups in order and use the traps table to catch the near-miss answers the test likes to offer.

Georgians

  • George I came from Germany and relied on his ministers. Robert Walpole was the first prime minister (1721 to 1742).
  • Culloden (1746): Bonnie Prince Charlie was defeated, the clans lost power, and the Highland Clearances followed. Robert Burns wrote Auld Lang Syne.
  • Enlightenment: Adam Smith (economics), David Hume (philosophy), James Watt (steam).
  • Industrial Revolution: Arkwright's factories, canals, then railways (Stephenson) and Bessemer steel. Working conditions were harsh.
  • Empire and trade: Cook mapped Australia, the East India Company took control of India, and sugar and tobacco flowed from the Caribbean. Dean Mahomed opened the first curry house in London (1810).
  • Slavery: Quakers and Wilberforce campaigned. The slave trade was banned in 1807, and slavery abolished in the Empire in 1833.
  • American independence: declared in 1776, recognised in 1783.
  • Napoleonic wars: Trafalgar (1805, Nelson killed) and Waterloo (1815, Wellington). The Act of Union with Ireland created the UK in 1801, and the Union Flag gained the cross of St Patrick.

Victorians (1837 to 1901)

  • The Empire grew to be the largest ever. Free trade followed the repeal of the Corn Laws (1846). The Factory Act limited women and children to 10 hours (1847).
  • Great Exhibition (1851) in the Crystal Palace. Brunel built the Great Western Railway and the Clifton Suspension Bridge. Britain produced over half the world's iron, coal and cotton cloth.
  • Crimean War (1853 to 1856): the Victoria Cross was introduced. Florence Nightingale transformed nursing.
  • Ireland: the potato famine killed about a million and sent another million and a half abroad. Parnell argued for home rule, and the Fenians for full independence.
  • Votes: the 1832 Reform Act ended rotten and pocket boroughs. Chartists pushed for more, and 1867 widened the vote. Women got property rights in 1870 and 1882.
  • Suffragettes: Emmeline Pankhurst founded the WSPU in 1903. Women over 30 voted from 1918, and at 21 from 1928.
  • Boer War (1899 to 1902) made people question the Empire. Kipling wrote about it.

Twentieth century

  • Early 1900s reforms: old-age pensions, free school meals, MPs' salaries.
  • First World War (1914 to 1918): triggered by the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand. Over 2 million British casualties and about 60,000 on the first day of the Somme (1916). It ended at 11 am on 11 November 1918.
  • Ireland: Easter Rising in 1916, then partition. The Irish Free State was created in 1922, and it became a republic in 1949. Northern Ireland stayed in the UK. The Troubles followed.
  • Between the wars: Great Depression (1929), BBC radio (1922), and television from 1936.
  • Second World War: Hitler invaded Poland in 1939. Churchill became prime minister in 1940. Dunkirk, the Battle of Britain (Spitfire and Hurricane), the Blitz, and D-Day (6 June 1944). Victory in Europe in May 1945, and against Japan in August 1945.
  • Post-war: Attlee's Labour government created the NHS (1948, Bevan) from the Beveridge Report and nationalised key industries. Independence for India and Pakistan (1947). Macmillan's "wind of change". Postwar migration brought workers from the West Indies, India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.
  • 1960s: social change and the Beatles. Equal pay and anti-discrimination laws for women.
  • Inventions: Baird (television), Watson-Watt (radar), Turing (computing theory), Fleming (penicillin), Crick (DNA), Whittle (jet engine), Cockerell (hovercraft), Goodfellow (cash machine, 1967), Edwards and Steptoe (IVF, 1978), Wilmut (Dolly the sheep, 1996), Mansfield (MRI), Berners-Lee (the web).
  • 1970s: strikes and economic problems. Direct rule in Northern Ireland from 1972.
  • Europe: the EEC began in 1957. The UK left the EU on 31 January 2020.
  • Thatcher (1979 to 1990): the first woman prime minister. Privatisation, union reforms, and the Falklands War (1982). John Major followed.
  • Blair (1997 to 2007): the Scottish Parliament and Welsh Assembly (now the Senedd) were created. The Good Friday Agreement was signed in 1998.
  • 2010 onwards: coalition under Cameron, the EU referendum on 23 June 2016 (51.9% to 48.1% to leave), then May and Johnson.

Exam traps: false or true?

Tempting answerThe truth
Wilberforce abolished slavery in 18071807 banned the slave trade. Slavery itself was abolished across the Empire in 1833.
The Union Flag has a Welsh dragonIt has three crosses. Wales was already united with England.
Walpole was an 18th-century monarchHe was the first prime minister.
Women got the vote on equal terms in 1918In 1918 only women over 30 could vote. Equal terms came in 1928 at age 21.
The suffragettes only wrote petitionsThey used civil disobedience, including chaining and arson, and hunger strikes.
Victory over Napoleon came at TrafalgarTrafalgar (1805) was a sea victory. Napoleon was beaten at Waterloo (1815).
Churchill won the 1945 electionHe lost it. Attlee's Labour won.
The NHS was created by ChurchillCreated in 1948 by Attlee's government, led by Aneurin Bevan.
Penicillin was invented by TuringFleming discovered it (1928). Turing is linked to computing.
Britain left the EU in 2016The referendum was in 2016. The UK formally left on 31 January 2020.
The whole of Ireland left the UK in 1922Six northern counties stayed as Northern Ireland.

Memory anchors

  • Two dates for slavery: "07 stops the trade, 33 sets them free."
  • Women's votes: "30 in 18, 21 in 28" (age and year).
  • Trafalgar is the sea, Waterloo is the land. Nelson and Wellington.
  • WWI ends at 11-11-18. The 11th hour of the 11th day of the 11th month.
  • Beveridge named the five giants: want, disease, ignorance, squalor, idleness.
  • Inventors: Baird sees (TV), Watt-Watson spots (radar), Fleming heals (penicillin), Berners-Lee connects (web).
  • 2016 votes, 2020 leaves: vote first, exit later.

Practice questions with answers

Ten questions from this chapter, with the correct answer and explanation shown. The full bank has 115 questions on this topic.

1. Who is generally regarded as the first British Prime Minister?

  • A. Oliver Cromwell
  • B. Sir Robert Walpole
  • C. Duke of Wellington
  • D. William Pitt

Answer: B. Sir Robert Walpole

Sir Robert Walpole, in office from 1721 to 1742, is generally regarded as the first British Prime Minister.

2. The Industrial Revolution began in Britain during which period?

  • A. The Roman period
  • B. The 12th century
  • C. The 20th century
  • D. The 18th and 19th centuries

Answer: D. The 18th and 19th centuries

Britain was the first country to industrialise, from the 18th century into the 19th century.

3. Who was British Prime Minister for most of the Second World War?

  • A. Clement Attlee
  • B. Stanley Baldwin
  • C. Neville Chamberlain
  • D. Winston Churchill

Answer: D. Winston Churchill

Winston Churchill became Prime Minister in 1940 and led Britain through most of the Second World War.

4. Sir Winston Churchill's famous wartime speeches were intended to:

  • A. Inspire national resolve and morale
  • B. Introduce rationing
  • C. Announce Britain's surrender
  • D. Declare war on the United States

Answer: A. Inspire national resolve and morale

Churchill's speeches were designed to rally the country and sustain morale during the darkest periods of the war.

5. Devolved governments were established in Scotland and Wales in:

  • A. 2009
  • B. 1979
  • C. 1999
  • D. 1989

Answer: C. 1999

Following referendums in 1997, the Scottish Parliament and the National Assembly for Wales first met in 1999.

6. What did the Great Reform Act of 1832 mainly do?

  • A. Abolished the House of Lords
  • B. Gave women the vote
  • C. Gave more men the vote and gave fast-growing towns representation in Parliament
  • D. Ended slavery in the Empire

Answer: C. Gave more men the vote and gave fast-growing towns representation in Parliament

The 1832 Reform Act extended the vote to more men and removed 'rotten boroughs', giving new industrial towns seats in Parliament.

7. Which of these was carried by Britain's canals during the Industrial Revolution?

  • A. Cars and lorries
  • B. Heavy goods such as coal and raw materials
  • C. Mail by air
  • D. Electricity

Answer: B. Heavy goods such as coal and raw materials

Canals were built to move heavy goods cheaply before railways spread. They carried coal, raw materials and manufactured goods.

8. What did a law of 1847 limit to 10 hours a day?

  • A. The working hours of women and children
  • B. The length of school days
  • C. Time spent in the army
  • D. The working hours of all men

Answer: A. The working hours of women and children

Working conditions in factories gradually improved, and in 1847 hours for women and children were limited by law.

9. Florence Nightingale is regarded as the founder of what?

  • A. Modern nursing
  • B. Modern medicine
  • C. The NHS
  • D. The Red Cross

Answer: A. Modern nursing

Nightingale improved hospital conditions in the Crimean War and set up the first nursing school at St Thomas' Hospital in 1860.

10. Which writer wrote The Jungle Book and the poem 'If'?

  • A. Robert Louis Stevenson
  • B. Rudyard Kipling
  • C. Thomas Hardy
  • D. Charles Dickens

Answer: B. Rudyard Kipling

Kipling was born in India and was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1907.
