A Modern, Thriving Society

This chapter covers how the UK looks and lives today: population, religion, festivals, sport, the arts and places to visit. It is the most fact-heavy chapter, so these notes group the facts the handbook states and flag the answers people mix up.

The UK today

  • The population is more diverse than 100 years ago. Nearly 10% have a parent or grandparent born abroad.
  • The longest mainland distance is John o'Groats to Land's End, about 870 miles (1,400 km).
  • Capitals: London, Edinburgh, Cardiff, Belfast.
  • Money: the pound sterling, 100 pence. Coins: 1p, 2p, 5p, 10p, 20p, 50p, £1, £2. Notes: £5, £10, £20, £50. Scottish and Northern Irish banknotes are valid across the UK, but shops can refuse them.
  • Languages: Welsh in Wales (taught in schools), Gaelic in parts of the Highlands and Islands, Irish Gaelic in Northern Ireland.
  • Population: about 4 million (1600), 8 million (1801), 20 million (1851), 40 million (1901), 50 million (1951), just over 66 million (2017). England is about 84%, Scotland just over 8%, Wales about 5%, Northern Ireland under 3%.
  • Ageing: people live longer, and there is a record number aged 85 and over, which affects pensions and health costs.
  • Equality: it is illegal to discriminate on gender or marital status. Women are about half the workforce.

Religion

  • 2011 census: 59% Christian, 4.8% Muslim, 1.5% Hindu, 0.8% Sikh, 25% no religion. 2021: 46% Christian, 6.5% Muslim.
  • You may choose any religion or none.
  • Church of England: the established church in England, a Protestant church dating from the 1530s. The monarch is its head, and the Archbishop of Canterbury is its spiritual leader. Some bishops sit in the House of Lords.
  • Church of Scotland: Presbyterian, run by ministers and elders, with a moderator chosen for one year. It is not a state church.
  • No established church in Wales or Northern Ireland. Other groups include Baptists, Methodists, Presbyterians, Quakers and Roman Catholics.

Saints' days

  • David, 1 March (Wales). Patrick, 17 March (Northern Ireland). George, 23 April (England). Andrew, 30 November (Scotland).
  • Only Scotland and Northern Ireland have these as official holidays.

Festivals and customs

  • Christmas Day (25 December) and Boxing Day are public holidays. Easter: Good Friday and Easter Monday are public holidays.
  • Lent is the 40 days before Easter. It starts on Ash Wednesday, and Shrove Tuesday (Pancake Day) is the day before.
  • Diwali: festival of lights, 5 days, October or November, Hindus and Sikhs. A famous celebration is in Leicester.
  • Hanukkah: 8 days, candles on a menorah.
  • Eid ul-Fitr: end of Ramadan. Eid ul-Adha: remembers Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son.
  • Vaisakhi: 14 April, founding of the Khalsa.
  • New Year: 1 January. In Scotland, Hogmanay is 31 December, and 2 January is also a holiday.
  • Valentine's Day 14 February. April Fool's Day 1 April (jokes until midday). Mothering Sunday is three weeks before Easter. Father's Day is the third Sunday in June.
  • Halloween 31 October, with pagan roots.
  • Bonfire Night 5 November, marking the failed Gunpowder Plot of 1605 led by Guy Fawkes.
  • Remembrance Day 11 November. Poppies are worn, there is a two-minute silence at 11 am, and wreaths are laid at the Cenotaph.
  • Bank holidays: early May, late May or early June, and August. In Northern Ireland the Battle of the Boyne anniversary in July is also a holiday.

Sport

  • The UK has hosted the Olympics three times: 1908, 1948 and 2012 (Stratford, East London). The 2012 Paralympics were also in London. The Paralympics began with Dr Ludwig Guttmann at Stoke Mandeville.
  • People: Roger Bannister (first sub-4-minute mile, 1954), Bobby Moore (1966 World Cup captain), Steve Redgrave (rowing, five golds), Tanni Grey-Thompson, Kelly Holmes (2004), Ellen MacArthur, Chris Hoy, Bradley Wiggins (first Briton to win the Tour de France, 2012), Mo Farah, Jessica Ennis-Hill, Andy Murray (Wimbledon 2013 and 2016).
  • Cricket: the Ashes (England v Australia). Football: the most popular sport, with England's only major title in 1966. Rugby: union and league, and the Six Nations (England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, France, Italy).
  • Horse racing: Royal Ascot, the Grand National (Aintree), the Scottish Grand National (Ayr). Golf: St Andrews, home of golf, and the Open. Tennis: Wimbledon, the oldest tournament and the only Grand Slam on grass.
  • Sailing: Francis Chichester (1966 to 67, single-handed), Robin Knox-Johnston (non-stop), and Cowes on the Isle of Wight. Motorsport: British winners include Damon Hill, Lewis Hamilton, Jenson Button.

Arts and culture

  • Music: the Proms (8 weeks, BBC, since 1927, Royal Albert Hall). Composers: Purcell, Handel (British citizen in 1727, Water Music, Messiah), Elgar (Pomp and Circumstance), Holst (The Planets), Vaughan Williams, Walton, Britten (Peter Grimes, the Aldeburgh Festival). Pop: the Beatles, Rolling Stones, punk. Festivals: Glastonbury, Isle of Wight, Creamfields, the Eisteddfod (Wales). Prizes: the Mercury Prize (September) and the Brit Awards.
  • Theatre: West End, The Mousetrap (since 1952), Gilbert and Sullivan, Andrew Lloyd Webber, pantomime (with the Dame), the Edinburgh Festival Fringe, and the Olivier Awards.
  • Art: Holbein and Van Dyck came from abroad. Gainsborough, Turner, Constable, the Pre-Raphaelites, Henry Moore, Lucian Freud, David Hockney. The Turner Prize began in 1984.
  • Architecture: Norman cathedrals and the White Tower, Inigo Jones, Wren, Robert Adam, Gothic Revival (Houses of Parliament, St Pancras), Lutyens (New Delhi, the Cenotaph), Foster, Rogers, Hadid. Gardens: Capability Brown, Gertrude Jekyll, and the Chelsea Flower Show.
  • Literature: Austen, Dickens (Scrooge, Micawber), Stevenson, Hardy, Conan Doyle, Amis, Greene, Rowling, Christie, Fleming. Tolkien's Lord of the Rings was voted best loved novel in 2003. Man Booker Prize since 1968. Poets: Beowulf, Chaucer, Shakespeare, Milton, Wordsworth, Blake, Keats, Byron, Shelley, Tennyson, Browning, Owen, Sassoon. Poets' Corner is in Westminster Abbey.
  • Film: first shown in 1896. Chaplin, Hitchcock, David Lean, Nick Park (Wallace and Gromit), Ealing Studios, BAFTA. Harry Potter and James Bond are the top-grossing franchises.
  • Comedy: Punch (1840s), Private Eye, music hall, Monty Python (1969), sitcoms and satire.
  • TV and radio: a TV licence is needed for anyone watching TV (fine up to £1,000). It funds the BBC. Over-75s can get a free licence and blind people a 50% discount. Coronation Street and EastEnders are soaps.

Leisure and daily life

  • Flowers: England the rose, Scotland the thistle, Wales the daffodil, Northern Ireland the shamrock.
  • Foods: England roast beef and Yorkshire pudding, Wales Welsh cakes, Scotland haggis, Northern Ireland the Ulster fry.
  • Pubs: 18 to buy alcohol. At 16 you may drink wine or beer with a meal with an adult.
  • Betting and the lottery: 18 and over.
  • Dogs: a collar with the owner's name and address, kept under control.
  • Gardens and allotments. Shops are open 7 days, with reduced hours on Sundays.

Places of interest

  • 15 national parks in England, Wales and Scotland. The Lake District is England's largest (912 sq mi), with Windermere the biggest lake. Snowdonia is in North Wales (Snowdon is the highest mountain in Wales). Loch Lomond and the Trossachs is in Scotland.
  • National Trust: founded in 1895 by three volunteers. Also the National Trust for Scotland.
  • Landmarks: Big Ben (the bell; the tower is Elizabeth Tower), the Eden Project (Cornwall), Edinburgh Castle, the Giant's Causeway (Northern Ireland, volcanic lava), the London Eye (135 m), the Tower of London (built by William the Conqueror, guarded by Yeoman Warders).

Remembrance Day is 5 NovemberIt is 11 November.
Diwali is MuslimIt is celebrated by Hindus and Sikhs.
Good Friday is the only Easter holidayEaster Monday is a holiday too.

Memory anchors

  • Saints: "David March first, Patrick March 17, George April 23, Andrew end of November."
  • Flowers: "Rose, Thistle, Daffodil, Shamrock", which spells RTDS, or England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland in order.
  • Olympics: "08, 48, 12", with 2012 at Stratford.
  • 11-11-11: Remembrance on 11 November, a silence at 11 am.
  • Bank holidays: "May, May/June, August."
  • 15 parks, 3 nations: England, Wales, Scotland (none in Northern Ireland in the handbook).
  • Wallace and Gromit = Nick Park.
  • Three A's of racing: Ascot, Aintree, Ayr. Wimbledon is the grass-court Grand Slam.

Practice questions with answers

Ten questions from this chapter, with the correct answer and explanation shown. The full bank has 135 questions on this topic.

1. When is St George's Day, the patron saint's day of England?

  • A. 17 March
  • B. 23 April
  • C. 1 March
  • D. 30 November

Answer: B. 23 April

St George's Day is celebrated on 23 April in England.

2. Remembrance Day, marking those who died in wars, is held on:

  • A. 11 November
  • B. 1 January
  • C. 5 November
  • D. 25 December

Answer: A. 11 November

Remembrance Day is 11 November, marking the end of the First World War in 1918. Poppies are worn.

3. Gilbert and Sullivan are best known for writing:

  • A. Detective novels
  • B. Symphonies
  • C. Landscape paintings
  • D. Comic operas

Answer: D. Comic operas

Gilbert and Sullivan wrote comic operas including HMS Pinafore and The Pirates of Penzance.

4. The minimum age for buying alcohol in a UK pub or shop is:

  • A. 18
  • B. 16
  • C. 21
  • D. 17

Answer: A. 18

You must be 18 or over to buy alcohol in the UK.

5. Which of these best describes the role of volunteering in UK society?

  • A. It is illegal
  • B. Only foreign nationals can volunteer
  • C. It is compulsory for all adults
  • D. It is widely encouraged and supports charities and communities

Answer: D. It is widely encouraged and supports charities and communities

Volunteering is common and valued in the UK, supporting charities, hospitals, schools and community groups.

6. Approximately how long is the longest distance on the UK mainland, from John o'Groats to Land's End?

  • A. About 1,500 miles
  • B. About 400 miles
  • C. About 200 miles
  • D. About 870 miles (1,400 km)

Answer: D. About 870 miles (1,400 km)

The longest distance from the north coast of Scotland to the southwest corner of England is about 870 miles.

7. Which patron saints' days are official holidays in the UK?

  • A. All four nations
  • B. England and Wales
  • C. Those of Scotland and Northern Ireland
  • D. Wales only

Answer: C. Those of Scotland and Northern Ireland

St Andrew's Day (30 November) and St Patrick's Day (17 March) are official holidays, though not all businesses in Scotland close.

8. Which two days around Easter are public holidays?

  • A. Shrove Tuesday and Easter Monday
  • B. Ash Wednesday and Easter Sunday
  • C. Good Friday and Easter Monday
  • D. Palm Sunday and Good Friday

Answer: C. Good Friday and Easter Monday

Good Friday marks the death of Jesus. Easter Sunday marks his rising.

9. Which composer is known for The Planets suite?

  • A. Edward Elgar
  • B. Benjamin Britten
  • C. Ralph Vaughan Williams
  • D. Gustav Holst

Answer: D. Gustav Holst

Holst's Jupiter is the tune for the hymn 'I Vow to Thee, My Country'.

10. Who is the English sculptor best known for large bronze abstract sculptures?

  • A. Sir John Lavery
  • B. Henry Moore
  • C. Lucian Freud
  • D. David Hockney

Answer: B. Henry Moore

Lucian Freud was known for portraits, and David Hockney contributed to pop art.
